**The practical way to handle after-hours service calls is a three-layer system: a live or AI answering layer that captures every call, a written triage script that separates true emergencies from tomorrow-morning jobs, and a dispatch workflow that turns captured calls into scheduled work instead of sticky notes. The right answering layer depends on your nightly call volume, ticket size, and trade.**

## Decide what after-hours calls are actually worth to you

**Before you buy anything, count your after-hours calls for two to four weeks and tag each one as emergency, routine, or junk. Most owners discover the volume is lower than feared but the emergency tickets are the highest-margin work they do. That math tells you whether you need coverage five nights a week or only on weekends.**

Pull your phone records or call log from your field service software. An illustrative example: a four-truck plumbing shop might log 11 after-hours calls a week, where three are true emergencies averaging a $650 ticket, five are routine requests that book the next day, and three are spam or price shoppers. If you miss all 11, you are not losing 11 jobs. You are losing the three emergencies and maybe two of the routine bookings.

Run that math before you compare vendors, because it sets the ceiling on what you should pay.

Two questions shape everything downstream:

- What share of after-hours calls are genuine emergencies worth a premium dispatch fee?
- What does one captured emergency job pay, on average, in your trade?

## Compare your three answering options honestly

**You have three realistic options: your own techs rotating on-call, a live answering service, or an AI front desk that answers and books. In-house costs nothing extra but burns out technicians. Answering services capture everything but charge per minute or per call. AI options cost less per call but need careful scripting for emergencies.**

Each option fails differently, and that failure mode matters more than the sticker price.

## Build the triage script before you pick a vendor

**Every answering option is only as good as the triage script behind it. The script is a short decision tree the agent, AI, or on-call tech follows: is anyone in danger, is property being damaged right now, or can this wait until morning. Write it once, test it monthly, and every coverage option gets dramatically better.**

A workable script has five checks:

1. **Safety check.** Gas smell, sparking panel, sewage backup into living space, no heat with an infant or elderly person in the home. These trigger immediate escalation to the on-call tech by phone, not text.
2. **Active damage check.** Water still running, door that will not secure. Escalate, but offer the premium after-hours rate first so the customer consents to the fee.
3. **Same-day vs next-day.** No hot water but the home is safe: offer the first morning slot and a confirmation text.
4. **Capture the details that matter.** Address, gate code, photos by text, equipment brand if the caller knows it, and whether they are the homeowner or a tenant.
5. **Set the callback expectation.** Tell the caller exactly when they will hear from a human, then hit that promise.

The [Locksmith Dispatch Software: Five After-Hours Checks](/guides/locksmith-dispatch-software/) guide uses this exact check-style framing for lockouts at 2 a.m., and the five-check structure transfers cleanly to plumbing, HVAC, and electrical. Whatever answering layer you choose, hand the vendor your script and test it with three mock calls before signing anything. Ask them to role-play a caller who is panicking and vague, because that is the call that exposes a weak script.

## Route the captured call into dispatch, not a notebook

**A captured call only becomes revenue when it lands on tomorrow's dispatch board with an address, a problem description, and a confirmed time window. Insist that your answering option writes directly into your scheduling or dispatch software, or at minimum sends a structured email and text. Verbal handoffs at 8 a.m. are where after-hours jobs die.**

The failure pattern looks like this: the answering service emails a message at 11:40 p.m., your office manager reads it at 7:30 a.m., calls the customer at 8:15, and the customer already booked a competitor who confirmed a window by text at 11:41 p.m. Speed of confirmation beats speed of callback for routine calls.

Wire the workflow so three things happen automatically:

- The call summary creates a draft job or booking request in your dispatch tool with the address and issue.
- The customer gets an immediate confirmation text with the promised window.
- The on-call tech gets escalation by phone call for emergencies, with a backup contact if the first tech does not acknowledge within a set number of minutes.

## Know when after-hours coverage is the wrong fix

**After-hours answering is the wrong investment when your real problem is pricing, capacity, or call quality, not missed calls. If you already answer most after-hours calls but rarely book them, more coverage changes nothing. Fix the dispatch fee, the callback speed, or the on-call rotation first, then buy coverage for the calls that remain.**

Three honest exceptions:

- **Your emergency pricing scares people off.** If callers hang up when they hear the after-hours rate, a better answering service just delivers more hangups. Reprice or reframe the fee first.
- **You have no one to send.** A one-man shop where the owner is already working 60 hours cannot sell 2 a.m. capacity. Answering the call and booking the job just creates a broken promise. In that case, use the answering layer to book first-morning slots only.
- **Most calls are price shoppers.** Some trades get after-hours calls that are mostly shoppers comparing quotes. Those convert poorly no matter who answers, so per-minute answering fees turn into a tax on junk volume.

Also watch the human cost of the in-house option. Rotating the on-call phone through techs looks free, but techs who get woken up three nights in a row start letting it ring, and then you are paying for nothing and losing the calls anyway. If your on-call tech's answer rate drops below roughly nine out of ten calls, the free option has stopped being free.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I use my own technicians or an answering service for after-hours calls?
Use your own techs if nightly volume is under about three calls and your ticket sizes justify waking someone up. Above that, or once techs start ignoring the phone, move routine capture to an answering service or AI layer and keep techs only for true emergency escalations.

### How much should I charge for an after-hours service call?
Set a dispatch fee that covers the tech's overtime plus a margin, and state it before you roll a truck. Many shops use a flat after-hours trip fee in the $150 to $300 range plus standard labor rates, but treat that as an illustrative range and price from your own overtime costs and market.

### Can an AI answering service handle real emergencies?
It can, but only with a tested escalation script. The AI must recognize emergency keywords, immediately phone the on-call tech, and have a backup contact if there is no answer. Test it monthly with mock calls. Never let an AI leave a flooding call in a message queue.

### What is the fastest way to start handling after-hours calls this week?
Forward your line after hours to a missed-call text-back or basic answering service, write a one-page triage script, and designate one on-call tech with a backup. That gets capture running in days while you evaluate fuller dispatch integration.

### How do I stop after-hours calls from burning out my techs?
Rotate the on-call phone weekly, pay a real stipend plus the overtime, cap consecutive on-call nights, and let an answering layer filter routine calls so techs only get woken for genuine emergencies. Burnout usually comes from junk calls, not emergencies.
